# Rhetoric, Structure, and Paragraph Mechanics (Direct IZA/Nikolov Compliance)

## 1. Paragraph & Sentence Architecture
- **The Rule of Oness:** Every single paragraph must adhere literally to *one* central analytical or empirical idea. If an argument drifts from topic X to topic Y within the paragraph, it must be split.
- **Inverted Pyramid & BLUF:** Enforce the Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF) pattern. Every paragraph must open with a comprehensive summary sentence that acts as an "umbrella," sheltering all subsequent empirical or theoretical points. Arrange subsequent sentences progressively from most significant to least significant.
- **Paragraph Length Constraint:** Restrict paragraphs strictly to a length of three to five sentences.
- **Sentence Simplicity:** Strive for short, clear sentences, utilizing monosyllabic words for simplicity wherever appropriate.

## 2. Textual Deadwood & Wordy Phrase Extraction
- **Sentence Stretchers:** Strip out all superfluous sentence stretchers that dilute empirical conviction, specifically blocking lines starting with "It is," "There is," or "There are".
- **The Banned Wordy Phrase Substitutions:** Scan prose and mandate the following direct cuts:
  * Replace "in order to" with "**to**".
  * Replace "whether or not" with "**whether**".
  * Replace "is equal to" with "**equals**".
- **The Isolated Pronoun "This" Ban:** Avoid starting sentences with the word "This" because it routinely induces ambiguity. If a sentence must start with "This," it *must* be followed immediately by a explicit noun specifying the reference (e.g., "This identification fragility", "This specification sensitivity").

## 3. Epistemological Tone & Scholarly Decorum
- **Adverb and Modifier Elimination:** Eliminate presumptive or dismissive adverbs. Completely ban the words "**clearly**," "**obviously**," and "**of course**". If an empirical finding is clear, its diagnostics will make it clear to the reader on their own. Strip the word "**very**" entirely.
- **Anti-Dramatic Clause:** Eradicate overly emotional or dramatic phrasing (e.g., "these results completely shatter our expectations" is too much). Use positive, evidence-based statements rather than normative claims about what "should" happen.
- **The Non-Economics Boundary:** Keep the main body of the text focused squarely on economics. Move broad political or sociocultural commentary out of the core analytical sections, keeping them strictly restricted to brief mentions in the introduction or conclusion. When on the fence about keeping non-economic material, leave it out.

## 4. Economic Tense, Voice, & Citation Norms
- **The Empirical Active Voice Rule:** Use active voice when writing about analytical estimations. "I estimated the model" must always be prioritized over "The model was estimated".
- **The Economic Historical Tense Rule:** Maintain a consistent present simple tense when discussing economic models, theories, or even when referencing past literature. Enforce: "Shaikh (2016) finds that..." over past tense formulations.
- **The Formal Citation Handshake:** In-text references must rely strictly on author surnames and dates. Never write full names or descriptive publishing contexts in the prose text. Enforce: "Mullainathan (2000) finds..." and completely reject lines like "Sendhil Mullainathan, in a 2006 journal article...".
- **Data Reporting Tone:** Accept a less-than-scintillating, repetitive delivery in the empirical sections as long as it prioritizes maximum clarity. Use direct phrases like "The results show," "The tables report," and "The estimated coefficient on" without hesitation.
