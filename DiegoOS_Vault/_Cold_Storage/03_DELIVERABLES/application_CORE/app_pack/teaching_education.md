# Teaching and Education Philosophy Statement

I teach economics as a way of asking disciplined questions about the world students already inhabit. My courses do not treat theory, evidence, institutions, and history as separate layers. They ask students to bring them into relation: to explain an economic outcome, identify the assumptions behind an argument, evaluate evidence, and recognize how power and distribution shape what economic categories mean in practice. That is also what draws me to CORE Econ. Its project begins from the premise that economics should help students reason about real problems, not merely acquire a closed technical vocabulary.

My teaching has developed around repeated analytical practice. Students rarely become careful economic thinkers through a single final paper or high-stakes exam. They develop that capacity by returning to the work of argument many times: reconstructing a claim, identifying the evidence behind it, comparing explanations, and learning how to revise. I therefore build courses with stable weekly rhythms, short writing assignments, transparent expectations, and staged feedback. In Introduction to Political Economy, short analytical papers and discussion posts made critical thinking a regular practice rather than an exceptional event. This structure is especially important in online and heterogeneous classrooms, where students enter with different levels of preparation, confidence, writing experience, and familiarity with economic language.

I also ask students to reason under constraint. Economic argument is not just a matter of applying the right concept. It requires judgment under limited evidence, competing explanations, institutional complexity, and historical specificity. My assignments reward coherence, disciplined use of concepts, evidence-based inference, and explicit recognition of limits. When I provide essay prompts in advance, for example, I am not lowering the level of difficulty. I am shifting the difficulty toward preparation, organization, and argument. Students know the question, but they still have to decide what matters, how evidence should be used, and where their interpretation is strongest or weakest.

I treat rigor as something that has to be designed. Demanding work is only fair when students can see how to enter it. I do not make difficult material easier by thinning it out; I make the route into it more legible. Across introductory economics, political economy, macroeconomics, and public policy research design, I pair demanding analytical tasks with low-stakes practice, reliable feedback, and clear expectations. This has allowed me to teach topics such as capitalism, inequality, development, macroeconomic instability, technological change, and institutional power without separating accessibility from analytical depth.

My experience teaching Econ 105, Introduction to Political Economy, is especially relevant here. As an Online Instructor at UMass UWW, I taught heterogeneous student cohorts using Bowles, Edwards, and Roosevelt’s *Understanding Capitalism*. The course framed capitalism through competition, command, and historical change, allowing students to analyze markets together with firms, governments, institutions, power, globalization, technological transformation, inequality, and conflict over income distribution. I do not present this as prior CORE teaching. The connection is pedagogical and substantive: the course treated economics as a way to investigate real-world problems, and it asked students to understand economic outcomes as historically situated and institutionally shaped.

That same logic shaped my graduate teaching in public policy and research design at Universidad de Santiago de Chile. Many students arrived with urgent policy concerns but uneven methodological preparation. My role was to help them turn broad concerns into feasible research questions, evidence strategies, and coherent empirical arguments. We worked through distinctions that often determine whether a project can actually be completed: a topic versus a question, a normative concern versus an analytical claim, a plausible mechanism versus an assumed causal relation. This is one of the places where my teaching and my research meet most directly. I want students to learn how economic problems become researchable without losing their social and institutional substance.

I approach mentorship in the same spirit. Advising is not an informal supplement to teaching; it requires structure, timing, and judgment. I work with students through early topic definition, staged milestones, explicit criteria, and predictable feedback. I have supervised undergraduate and master’s projects, including M.A. Public Policy work on public procurement governance, municipal corruption, and feminist political economy. In these projects, I helped students clarify questions, define evidence strategies, revise arguments, and complete demanding empirical work. Student feedback has consistently emphasized organization, clarity, reliability, technical support, and dialogic supervision. For me, that pattern confirms that high standards and strong support do not pull in opposite directions.

My recent teaching has also incorporated digital pedagogy and AI literacy. I designed a course on Generative AI and Digital Capitalism that treated AI both as an object of political-economic analysis and as a tool whose use requires judgment. I do not approach AI in teaching as something to either celebrate or ban. I ask students to use it with transparency, verification, source checking, and intellectual accountability. AI can support synthesis, questioning, comparison, and revision, but it cannot replace the student’s responsibility for interpretation and argument. The central task is to preserve student ownership of analytical work while teaching the digital literacies now required for serious inquiry.

This is where I see a concrete contribution to CORE Econ’s pedagogical innovation agenda. I would be well positioned to help develop an interactive, AI-assisted Research Design Module for heterogeneous public policy students. The module would help students move from real-world economic problems to researchable questions, critical literature synthesis, conceptual frameworks, iterative hypotheses, and evidence strategies. Its AI component would be governed by a simple rule: AI may assist exploration, comparison, translation, and synthesis, but students must verify claims, evaluate evidence, and construct the final argument themselves. In practice, this could be built through structured reading paths, research-design templates, AI-use protocols, instructor-facing guides, and student-facing exercises linked to CORE’s problem-based materials.

The broader purpose is to make economics education accessible without making it thin. Students should learn to move from a real-world problem to an analytical question, from a question to evidence, and from evidence to a defensible argument. My teaching experience has prepared me to contribute to CORE Econ not only as an instructor, but as a curriculum builder who can design structured, evidence-based, and AI-literate learning materials for diverse students. I would bring to this work a political economist’s concern with inequality and institutions, a teacher’s commitment to scaffolding and feedback, and a practical orientation toward responsible digital innovation.
