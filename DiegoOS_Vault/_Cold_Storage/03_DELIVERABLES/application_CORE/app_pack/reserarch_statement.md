# Research Statement  
## Historical Political Economy of Accumulation, Distribution, and Institutional Constraint

My research is in historical political economy. I examine how capitalist economies build productive capacity, organize distribution, and confront institutional and external constraints across uneven positions in the global system. Macroeconomic categories such as capacity utilization, output gaps, external balances, productivity, and financial stability often appear as neutral measures. In practice, they are formed through distributive conflict, labor relations, state institutions, and core-periphery hierarchies. My work reconstructs those categories historically and empirically to show how they shape accumulation, crisis, and inequality.

I work across classical political economy, heterodox macroeconomics, and applied empirical research. I use time-series econometrics, historical data construction, comparative-historical analysis, and structured qualitative coding to examine how economic relations become measurable, contested, and politically consequential. This approach aligns with the Stone Centre’s commitment to placing inequality at the center of economics and with CORE Econ’s emphasis on problem-based, empirically grounded teaching.

## Dissertation Research

My dissertation, *Capacity Utilization and Accumulation Regimes: Essays in the Political Economy of Growth, Distribution, and Core-Periphery Dynamics*, examines capacity utilization as a relation between accumulation, productive capacity, distribution, and external constraint. Rather than treating utilization as a neutral technical baseline, a simple output-gap proxy, or a unified firm-level target, I show how it is shaped by institutions, class conflict, and the position of economies within the world system.

The first chapter develops this argument through a critical replication of Anwar Shaikh’s capacity-utilization measure. I begin from the econometric relation between output and capital, but I shift the emphasis from recovering a single utilization series to identifying the conditions under which that relation can be sustained. I reinterpret the output-capital coefficient as a transformation elasticity linking capital accumulation to the formation of productive capacity. The replication shows that a Shaikh-like utilization path can be reproduced in a narrow single-equation setting. Once the specification space is widened, however, the result becomes fragile. In the system setting, the output-capital relation becomes stable only when the logged rate of exploitation enters the long-run relation. This finding is not merely econometric. It shows that productive capacity cannot be identified independently of distributional conflict.

The second chapter develops the theoretical implications of that result. Standard accounts often assume that firms can define and pursue a coherent “normal” rate of utilization. I challenge this premise by bringing together Okishio’s analysis of market disproportionality and Matt Vidal’s theory of the internally divided firm. The firm is not a unified subject smoothly optimizing capacity. It is an organization divided by managerial imperatives, labor resistance, and competitive pressure. Utilization is therefore unstable: it reflects the tension between labor control, market anarchy, and accumulation. Extending this argument to center-periphery relations clarifies an additional asymmetry. All capitalist economies face the accumulation-capacity problem, but peripheral economies confront it under foreign exchange scarcity, imported capital-goods dependence, technological subordination, and external financial constraint.

The third chapter applies this framework to Chile’s Unidad Popular. I reconstruct that conjuncture as one in which external financial subordination, class struggle, and the breakdown of macroeconomic reproduction became inseparable. I introduce a central-bank leverage ratio to capture the point at which domestic monetary liabilities and access to world money converge. Using historical political economy and applied time-series evidence, I trace how external shocks moved through international demand, copper prices, and the terms of trade to reshape the central bank’s leverage position. This mechanism defined the external corridor within which domestic class conflict unfolded. The crisis appears not as a simple policy failure, but as a historically specific interaction between international constraint, state capacity, and distributive struggle.

Together, these chapters establish the central claim of my dissertation: foundational macroeconomic measures do not simply record economic reality. They are built within historical relations of conflict, institution-building, and hierarchy. My broader research extends this claim through work on rural-urban transition and labor discipline in Chile’s developmental state, teacher education segregation and the reproduction of inequality, gender gaps in labor-market participation, structured qualitative coding, and historical data infrastructure. Across these projects, I analyze inequality not as a residual outcome, but as part of the institutional and macroeconomic relations through which capitalist development is organized.

## Future Research

I will develop this research program along three lines: macroeconomic measurement and distribution; distributive cycles, financial fragility, and wealth-inequality pressures; and land rent, financialization, and peripheral accumulation.

The first line of work extends my dissertation research on macroeconomic measurement. I will examine how measures of capacity, slack, and productive potential change once distributional conflict is included in the analysis. In the short term, I plan to develop three articles from the dissertation: one on the fragility of capacity-utilization measures under alternative time-series specifications; one on the internally divided firm and the instability of “normal” utilization; and one on external constraint and class conflict during Chile’s Unidad Popular.

This work contributes to political economy, heterodox macroeconomics, economic methodology, and Latin American historical political economy. Its publication strategy is clear because each paper connects a precise measurement problem to a broader theoretical claim: macroeconomic indicators cannot be separated from the distributional and institutional relations that make them meaningful. The project is also well suited to inequality centers, political economy institutes, and historical social science programs because it links technical measurement to distribution, state capacity, and policy constraint. If one empirical article proves too narrow for a given outlet, the theoretical and historical components can still stand as independent publications.

The second line of work extends my analysis of functional distribution into macro-financial dynamics. Building on my current Goodwin-Minsky working paper, I will examine how distributive cycles, debt dynamics, and financial fragility generate wealth-inequality pressures. My entry point is not household wealth inequality in isolation. I focus on how wage-profit conflict, financial instability, and balance-sheet positions shape the divergence between vulnerability and asset ownership.

This project provides a direct bridge to the Stone Centre’s concern with wealth inequality while preserving the macro-political economy core of my research. It can generate a formal paper on distributive cycles and financial fragility, an empirical paper linking distributional shifts to debt and asset-price dynamics, and teaching-facing materials that help students understand how inequality operates through both income flows and balance sheets. If household-level wealth data prove too restrictive, I can pursue the question through sectoral balance sheets, macro-financial aggregates, and historical episodes of credit expansion and crisis. The project does not depend on a single dataset. Its core question is how distributional conflict becomes financially amplified.

The third line of work expands my research into urban and spatial political economy. In my current postdoctoral collaboration on land prices, land use, localization rents, and financialization in Latin America, I am developing a model that connects Minskyan financial fragility with classical political economy approaches to land rent. I will build this into a comparative project that begins with Chile and extends toward Latin American metropolitan economies.

This research examines how financialization reorganizes land, rent, and urban development in peripheral capitalism. It connects directly to inequality because land rents and asset-price dynamics shape access to urban space, redistribute gains across classes and institutions, and mediate the relation between global finance and local development. The project can support a theoretical article on rent and financial fragility, an empirical Chilean case, and a comparative Latin American extension. If cross-national data prove uneven, the Chilean case can advance through administrative data, local institutional analysis, and staged comparison with additional metropolitan areas.

## Fit with CORE Econ and the Stone Centre

The CORE Econ / Stone Centre fellowship is an especially strong setting for this research because it joins inequality research with the translation of economics into public-facing teaching materials. My work contributes to the Stone Centre network by bringing historical political economy to questions of functional distribution, macroeconomic measurement, external constraint, financial fragility, and rent. I do not treat inequality as an add-on to economics. I examine how inequality shapes accumulation, crisis, state capacity, and the categories economists use to interpret the economy.

My research also speaks to CORE Econ because its questions are teachable. Why do output gaps and capacity measures become fragile? How does distribution affect accumulation? Why do peripheral economies face external constraints differently from core economies? How do land, finance, and institutions transmit inequality? How do measurement choices shape policy possibilities? These questions allow students to connect theory, evidence, institutions, and public policy without reducing economics to technique.

In the next stage of my work, I will consolidate a research program in historical political economy that is technically rigorous, institutionally grounded, and pedagogically useful. I will continue developing applied econometric research on distribution and accumulation, extend my macro-financial work on wealth-inequality pressures, and build a comparative agenda on land rent and financialization in Latin America. Across these projects, I ask how capitalist economies organize the conditions of accumulation, and how those conditions become unstable through the inequalities they produce.
