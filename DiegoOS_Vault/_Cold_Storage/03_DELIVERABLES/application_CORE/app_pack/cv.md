**Dissertation:** *Capacity Utilization and Accumulation Regimes: Essays in the Political Economy of Growth, Distribution, and Core-Periphery Dynamics*

**Dissertation abstract:**  
This dissertation investigates capacity utilization as a constructed object of political economy: not a neutral technical baseline, not a simple output-gap proxy, and not a unified firm-level behavioral target, but a historically mediated relation between accumulation, productive capacity, distribution, and external constraints. Across three linked essays, it examines how capitalist economies construct, strain, and destabilize productive capacity across different structural positions in the global capitalist system. Chapter 1 critically replicates Shaikh’s capacity-utilization measure and shows that valid capacity identification requires distributional conflict and institutional shock vectors inside the structural estimation architecture. Chapter 2 reconstructs normal utilization through the internally divided firm, market anarchy, and the differentiated constraints of center and periphery. Chapter 3 applies this framework to Chile’s Unidad Popular, tracing how external financial subordination, central-bank balance-sheet dynamics, and class conflict shaped the limits of macroeconomic reproduction. The dissertation argues that capacity utilization and accumulation regimes are fundamentally driven by distributive conflict, labor relations, and core-periphery hierarchies.

2. Internally Divided Firm and Market Anarchy: Growth and Distribution in Center and Periphery
