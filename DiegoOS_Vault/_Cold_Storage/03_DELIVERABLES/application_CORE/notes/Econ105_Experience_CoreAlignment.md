# Econ 105 and CORE Econ Alignment Note

## One-Sentence Claim

My experience teaching introductory political economy demonstrates a strong pedagogical and substantive alignment with CORE Econ’s mission to teach economics through real-world, empirically grounded problem-solving.

## Teaching Context

As an Online Instructor for Econ 105, Introduction to Political Economy, at UMass UWW, I taught heterogeneous student cohorts across multiple terms, including Summer Session 2 2021, Fall 2021, and Spring 2024.

The course served as a foundational introduction to U.S. and global economies. It required a course architecture capable of supporting students with diverse backgrounds while maintaining rigorous analytical standards.

For application purposes, Econ 105 should be presented as evidence of my capacity to teach introductory economics in a way that is accessible, structured, institutionally grounded, and analytically demanding.

## Textbook Framework

The primary textbook for the course, Bowles, Edwards, and Roosevelt’s *Understanding Capitalism: Competition, Command, and Change*, provides a natural bridge to the CORE Econ project.

The book frames economics not only through competition in markets, but also through command within firms, governments, and international relations, and through change as a permanent feature of capitalist development.

This framework allowed me to teach economics as a discipline concerned with:

- markets and competition
- firms, authority, and command
- institutions and power
- globalization
- technological change
- inequality
- conflict over income distribution
- historical transformation

This orientation differs from a narrow presentation of economics as only the study of market exchange or abstract model-building. It gave students a way to understand economic outcomes as historically situated, institutionally mediated, and shaped by distributional conflict.

## CORE Econ Alignment

The connection to CORE Econ should be framed as pedagogical and substantive alignment, not as prior formal use of CORE materials.

My teaching of Econ 105 aligns with CORE Econ because both approaches treat economics as a way to understand real-world problems. The relevant connection is not that I taught CORE textbooks directly, but that I taught introductory economics through a framework that foregrounds inequality, institutions, power, historical change, globalization, technological transformation, and evidence-based reasoning.

This is especially important for the CORE application because the position requires more than disciplinary expertise. It requires the ability to translate complex economic ideas into accessible materials for undergraduate and public-policy audiences.

Econ 105 demonstrates that I already have experience teaching introductory economics in this mode: problem-oriented, historically grounded, inequality-conscious, and accessible to students with heterogeneous preparation.

## Teaching Practice

My instructional practice translated these complex themes into a structured online learning environment.

Rather than relying only on high-stakes testing, I organized the course around predictable weekly rhythms, short analytical writing cycles, online discussion, and regular feedback. This structure helped students practice critical thinking repeatedly rather than encountering it only in final assignments or exams.

The underlying pedagogical principle was high-support rigor. I treated rigor as a design problem: demanding intellectual work must be paired with visible scaffolding, low-stakes practice spaces, and reliable feedback.

Through this structure, students were trained to construct clear, evidence-based arguments under constraint. They learned to connect economic concepts to institutions, historical change, inequality, and power relations in a disciplined way.

## Reusable Application Paragraph

My approach to teaching introductory economics aligns closely with CORE Econ’s mission to broaden economics education through real-world, problem-based learning. As an Online Instructor for Introduction to Political Economy, I taught heterogeneous student cohorts through Bowles, Edwards, and Roosevelt’s *Understanding Capitalism*, a text that frames capitalism through competition, command, and historical change. This allowed me to introduce students to economics as a discipline concerned with inequality, institutional power, globalization, technological transformation, and conflict over income distribution. Because I teach students with diverse levels of preparation, I treat rigor as a design problem: I use structured analytical progression, short writing cycles, online discussion, and reliable feedback to make complex political economy concepts accessible without reducing their depth. This experience prepares me to contribute to CORE Econ’s pedagogical materials for undergraduate and public-policy audiences.

## Wording Cautions

Do not claim that I taught CORE Econ materials directly unless this is explicitly supported elsewhere.

Avoid saying:

> I used CORE Econ in Econ 105.

Safer formulation:

> My Econ 105 teaching was pedagogically and substantively aligned with CORE Econ’s approach to real-world, problem-based economics education.

Do not conflate Econ 105 with graduate public policy teaching. Econ 105 demonstrates online introductory teaching for heterogeneous student cohorts. Public policy teaching and mentorship occurred separately at USACH.

Do not present the “competition, command, and change” framework as my own invention. Attribute it clearly to Bowles, Edwards, and Roosevelt’s *Understanding Capitalism*.

Be careful with timeline claims. The teaching portfolio may explicitly list Econ 105 for Summer 2021 and Fall 2021, while Spring 2024 may need confirmation depending on the CV or portfolio version used. In final application documents, either verify Spring 2024 or use a more general formulation such as:

> across multiple terms, including 2021 and 2024

Avoid overstating the CORE connection. Do not say the course “directly mirrors” CORE Econ. Prefer:

> shares a strong pedagogical and substantive affinity with CORE Econ

or:

> provides a natural bridge to CORE Econ’s approach.
