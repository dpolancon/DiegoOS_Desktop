# CORE Econ — Pedagogical Innovation Pitch

## Core Claim

My strongest pedagogical innovation pitch for the CORE Econ application is the development of an interactive, AI-assisted research design module for heterogeneous public policy students.

The proposed module would bridge CORE’s problem-based economics materials with the practical research-design scaffolding students need to formulate empirical questions, evaluate evidence, synthesize literature, and design feasible policy-oriented research projects.

## Strategic Opportunity

CORE Econ has developed highly accessible and problem-oriented teaching materials for undergraduate economics and public policy audiences. The opportunity I can address is not to replace those materials, but to extend them with a more explicit research-design layer.

The relevant gap is the transition from learning economics through real-world problems to independently designing empirical research about those problems.

This is especially important for public policy students, who often bring rich professional and disciplinary backgrounds but uneven preparation in economics, empirical methods, and research design.

For the application, the strategic claim should be:

> CORE’s public-policy-facing materials can be strengthened by an interactive research design pathway that helps heterogeneous students move from economic problems to researchable questions, evidence strategies, and disciplined empirical argumentation.

## Proposed Contribution

I can propose developing a digitally native Research Design Module that could complement materials such as *Economy, Society, and Public Policy* and *Doing Economics*.

The module would help students move through four stages:

1. Formulating feasible research questions
2. Building theoretical or conceptual frameworks
3. Conducting critical literature synthesis
4. Developing iterative hypotheses and evidence strategies

The module should be framed as a pedagogical bridge between economic reasoning and independent empirical inquiry.

## Public Policy Teaching Foundation

This proposal draws directly on my experience teaching research design to public policy students.

In my public policy teaching, I have trained students to move from broad policy concerns to precise and feasible research questions. This requires helping students avoid vague problem statements, premature causal claims, and research designs that are too ambitious for the available evidence.

The CORE-relevant point is that this experience is not only methodological. It is pedagogical. It shows that I can design structured learning pathways for students who need to connect economic concepts, policy problems, and empirical evidence.

## Module Components

### 1. Research Question Formation

The first component would guide students in narrowing broad policy interests into specific, researchable questions.

It would teach students to distinguish between:

- a topic and a question
- a policy concern and an empirical object
- a normative claim and an analytical claim
- a plausible mechanism and an assumed causal relation

This component would be especially useful for public policy students who often begin with urgent real-world problems but need support translating them into feasible research designs.

### 2. Iterative Hypothesis Development

The second component would teach students to treat hypotheses as revisable analytical tools rather than fixed guesses.

Students often make early assumptions that shape the rest of the project. If those assumptions are weak, the whole project can inherit the initial error.

The module would therefore teach iterative hypothesis development: students would refine their hypotheses as they encounter literature, data, institutional context, and methodological constraints.

### 3. Theoretical and Conceptual Frameworks

The third component would help students distinguish between theoretical frameworks and conceptual frameworks.

A theoretical framework is useful when a project tests or applies an established body of theory.

A conceptual framework is more appropriate when a project is exploratory, inductive, or oriented toward mapping a policy problem whose causal structure is not yet fully specified.

This distinction is useful for heterogeneous public policy classrooms because students often need flexible but disciplined tools for organizing complex empirical problems.

### 4. Critical Literature Synthesis

The fourth component would train students to move beyond descriptive literature reviews.

Instead of summarizing paper after paper, students would learn to synthesize findings, compare methods, identify empirical gaps, and position their own question within a field of debate.

This component would directly support CORE’s emphasis on evidence-based reasoning and problem-oriented economics education.

## AI-Assisted Learning Tools

The role description’s emphasis on AI-assisted learning creates a strong opening for my digital pedagogy profile.

My proposal would not treat AI as a shortcut for student writing. It would treat AI as a bounded learning tool that supports synthesis, questioning, and verification while preserving student ownership of the argument.

The module could include AI-assisted exercises for:

- summarizing bodies of literature
- comparing competing explanations
- identifying missing variables or mechanisms
- generating counterarguments
- stress-testing research questions
- mapping evidence gaps
- translating technical material into accessible language

The governing principle should be AI literacy, not AI substitution.

## AI Hygiene Framework

The AI component should be framed through an “AI hygiene” approach.

This means students would learn to use AI tools under explicit rules of transparency, verification, and intellectual accountability.

The workflow can be summarized as:

1. Imagine: use AI to explore possibilities, alternative framings, and adjacent questions.
2. Discern: evaluate, verify, and reject weak or unsupported outputs.
3. Integrate: incorporate only those elements that survive independent judgment and source checking.

This framework prevents AI from replacing the student’s analytical work. It positions AI as an auxiliary tool for disciplined inquiry.

## Protected Analytical Space

A key design principle is the protection of the student’s own analytical space.

Students should not outsource the formulation of their central argument, hypothesis, or interpretation. Those elements must remain under their control.

AI can support literature navigation, comparison, synthesis, and revision. But the module should clearly separate AI-assisted exploration from the student’s final argumentative responsibility.

This is particularly important in public policy education, where students must learn not only to produce outputs, but to defend the reasoning behind those outputs.

## Digital Architecture

The module should be digitally native rather than merely a static PDF.

My experience with Markdown, Obsidian, structured notes, and non-linear knowledge organization gives me a concrete basis for contributing to this kind of resource.

The module could be organized through:

- short reading paths
- optional deeper routes
- concept maps
- research-design checklists
- staged exercises
- interactive prompts
- AI-use protocols
- instructor-facing guides
- student-facing templates

This architecture would allow students with different backgrounds to enter the material at different levels while still moving toward a common research-design standard.

## Reading Path Model

A useful structure would be a layered reading-path model.

Example:

### 20–30 Minute Path

For students who need the core intuition:

- What is a research question?
- What makes a question feasible?
- What kind of evidence would answer it?
- What are the main risks in the design?

### Standard Path

For students developing an applied project:

- Research question
- Literature synthesis
- Conceptual framework
- Hypothesis refinement
- Evidence strategy
- Limitations

### Deeper Route

For advanced students:

- Causal identification
- Measurement problems
- Data limitations
- Mixed-methods designs
- Replication and robustness
- AI-assisted synthesis protocols

This format would make the module scalable across undergraduate, public policy, and advanced applied settings.
