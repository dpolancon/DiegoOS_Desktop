## Selection Logic

The application should be calibrated around four evaluative dimensions:

- **Heterodox & Pluralist Fit:** The candidate must not sound like a strict neoclassical economist. They must demonstrate an appreciation for multiple economic paradigms, critical political economy, or heterodox methodologies.
    
- **Alignment with the Seven Principles:** The teaching and diversity statements must not just use buzzwords; they must show a practical, pedagogical understanding of John Jay’s specific framework (e.g., how "Democratic Education" looks in an economics classroom, or how "Equitable Assessment" is applied in quantitative courses).
    
- **Contextual Awareness (John Jay's Mission):** The candidate must understand the unique context of John Jay College. They need to show enthusiasm for teaching students who are deeply engaged with issues of criminal justice, social work, and public administration, many of whom are non-traditional or first-generation.
    
- **Institutional Citizenship:** The search committee wants a colleague who will help "enhance department culture." The candidate must project collegiality, a willingness to do the unglamorous work of course coordination, and a commitment to shared governance and community service.

## Fit Architecture

- **Core fit:** Heterodox economics, pluralism, critical political economy, and applied microeconomics focused on inequality, labor, or criminal justice.
    
- **Research bridge:**
    - Frame economic research not just as the study of resource allocation, but as the study of power, institutions, and social outcomes.
    - Highlight research that interrogates institutional racism, the economics of the criminal justice system, or the material conditions of marginalized communities.
        
- **Teaching bridge:**
    - Directly map teaching philosophy to the Seven Principles. 
    - *Example:* "In my Econometrics course, I practice *Democratic Education* by having students select their own empirical projects related to local NYC social issues, and I use *Diverse Content* by incorporating data and case studies from the Global South and marginalized US communities."
    - Emphasize *Embodied Learning* by acknowledging the lived experiences students bring to the classroom and using trauma-informed practices in high-stress quantitative courses.
        
- **Inclusion bridge:**
    - *Research:* Democratizing knowledge by publishing in accessible formats or engaging in community-based participatory research (CBPR).
    - *Teaching:* Designing *Equitable Assessments* that allow students to demonstrate mastery in multiple ways, reducing the anxiety often associated with traditional economics exams.
    - *Service:* Active mentoring of first-generation students, participating in CUNY-wide diversity initiatives, and engaging in community service in NYC.

## Main Risks

- **Risk 1: Sounding too mainstream/neoclassical.**
    - _Mitigation:_ Explicitly use the terms "heterodox," "pluralist," and "critical." Highlight any teaching or research that departs from strict neoclassical assumptions. If your background is mainstream, emphasize your methodological pluralism and openness to heterodox perspectives.
        
- **Risk 2: Treating the Seven Principles as a generic checklist.**
    - _Mitigation:_ Do not just list the 7 principles. Explain *how* you implement them. Give concrete examples of what "Equitable Assessment" looks like in your specific economics courses, or how you facilitate "Critical Engagement" when discussing controversial economic policies.
        
- **Risk 3: Ignoring the specific student demographic at John Jay.**
    - _Mitigation:_ John Jay students are uniquely focused on criminal justice, law, and social services. Tailor your teaching examples to show how you make economics relevant to these specific career paths and interests. Acknowledge the high proportion of first-generation and transfer students.
        
- **Risk 4: Underplaying the importance of service and department culture.**
    - _Mitigation:_ John Jay explicitly mentions interacting effectively with colleagues to "enhance department culture." Ensure your cover letter and DEI statement highlight your collaborative nature, willingness to coordinate courses, and commitment to shared governance.
